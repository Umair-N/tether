● Lighthouses have guided ships for more than two thousand years. The most famous early example was the Pharos of
  Alexandria, built around 280 BCE. It stood on an island near the Egyptian coast. Its fire burned at the top of a tall
  stone tower, and sailors could see it from far away at sea. The Pharos became one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient
  World. Its name later gave several languages their word for lighthouse, such as the Italian faro.

  Early lighthouses burned open fires, usually of wood or coal. Keepers had to feed the flames through the night. Wind
  and rain often put the fires out, so the work was hard and sometimes dangerous. In the 1700s, builders began to use
  oil lamps with curved mirrors behind them. These reflectors sent the light outward in a narrower beam across the
  water. Later, the Fresnel lens, first used in 1822, used rings of glass to bend light much farther. Ships could then
  see a strong beam from many miles away.

  Each lighthouse had its own signal, so sailors could tell where they were. Some lights flashed in set patterns. Others
  turned slowly, and some stayed fixed. Mariners learned these patterns and checked them against their charts. Keepers
  lived in the towers or in cottages nearby. They cleaned the lenses, wound the clockwork that turned the light, and
  kept logs of the weather. Their work was lonely, and many families lived with them on remote rocks.

  Today most lighthouses run on electricity and need little human care. Satellites and GPS now help ships find their
  way, so many lights have been switched off or turned into museums. Even so, people still love them. Lighthouses appear
  in paintings, poems, and novels, and they often stand as symbols of hope. Visitors climb the spiral stairs to see the
  view and learn about the keepers who once lived there. A lighthouse, even a silent one, still tells a story about
  courage and care.

● Tides are the regular rise and fall of the sea along the coast. Most coasts get two high tides and two low tides each
  day. The cause is gravity, mostly from the Moon. The Moon pulls on the Earth, and it pulls hardest on the side that
  faces it. This pull lifts the water there into a bulge. A second bulge forms on the far side of the Earth, where the
  Moon's pull is weakest.

  As the Earth turns, each coast passes through both bulges. This is why most places see two high tides in about one
  day. The cycle actually takes about 24 hours and 50 minutes, because the Moon also moves along its orbit. So high tide
