● Tides are the regular rise and fall of the sea along the coast. Most coasts get two high tides and two low tides each
  day. The cause is gravity, mostly from the Moon. The Moon pulls on the Earth, and it pulls hardest on the side that
  faces it. This pull lifts the water there into a bulge. A second bulge forms on the far side of the Earth, where the
  Moon's pull is weakest.

  As the Earth turns, each coast passes through both bulges. This is why most places see two high tides in about one
  day. The cycle actually takes about 24 hours and 50 minutes, because the Moon also moves along its orbit. So high tide
  comes about 50 minutes later each day. The Sun also pulls on the oceans, but its effect is less than half as strong
  as the Moon's. When the Sun and Moon line up, at new moon and full moon, their pulls add together. The result is a
  spring tide, with very high highs and very low lows. When they pull at right angles, at the quarter moons, the result
  is a weaker neap tide.

  The shape of the land changes tides a lot. In the open ocean, the tide may rise less than one meter. In narrow bays,
  the water can pile up much higher. The Bay of Fundy in Canada has the largest tides in the world, with a range of
  about 16 meters. Tides matter to people and to nature. Sailors plan trips around them, and fishers follow the fish
  they bring. Tide pools hold crabs, snails, and sea stars that live between the marks of high and low water. Some
  countries now use tidal power stations to make electricity from the moving water.
